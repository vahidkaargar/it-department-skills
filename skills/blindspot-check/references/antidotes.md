# Antidotes — debiasing tools to deploy against the biases

After naming which biases are live, prescribe one or two of these as the fix. Each entry: **Tool** — what it is — why it works.

- **Pre-Mortem** — Instead of asking "what might go wrong?" after a project fails, you imagine it has already failed and work backwards to identify causes. — By reframing as hindsight, teams surface vulnerabilities otherwise ignored. It improves planning under uncertainty.
- **Steelman** — The steelman is the opposite of the strawman: instead of weakening an opponent's argument into a flimsy caricature, you rebuild it into the strongest, most compelling version possible - sometimes even stronger than the original - then engage with that. — Steelman forces humility and rigor. By seeking the strongest case against your own position you preempt obvious rebuttals, expose blind spots, and build trust. Like Bayesian updating, testing against the strongest counterview maximizes your learning signal.
- **Devil's Advocate** — The structured role of someone to argue the opposite view in order to stress-test reasoning. — Superforecasters use it to combat groupthink and confirmation bias. It builds deliberate dissent into forecasting.
- **Jacobi's Inversion Principle** — Charlie Munger’s favorite mental model: “Invert, always invert.” Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how to fail — then avoid those traps. — Success often comes from avoiding stupidity, not pursuing brilliance. Many problems are easier solved backward. It converts open-ended goals into concrete error-prevention.
- **Bull a. Bear List** — A structured pro/con list for an investment: bull case vs. bear case. — Forces investors to articulate both upside and downside in a balanced way. It reduces one-sided thinking.
- **Consensus-Contrarian Matrix** — Howard Marks frames outcomes along two dimensions: whether your view is consensus or contrarian, and whether it's right or wrong. The lesson: you only earn outsized returns by being contrarian and right. — Consensus + right = average returns (already priced in). Consensus + wrong = you lose, but with company. Contrarian + wrong = you lose, often painfully. Contrarian + right = you capture alpha. It disciplines thinking about why you deserve to win.
- **Pre-Consensus vs. Post-Consensus** — A timing lens: the same idea or trade looks very different before consensus forms versus after consensus forms. The biggest opportunities lie in pre-consensus recognition, while post-consensus is usually just harvesting. — Pre-consensus: few believe, prices are low, ridicule is common; risk feels high but reward is asymmetric. Post-consensus: everyone believes, prices reflect it, risk of disappointment rises. Opportunity ~ the delta between belief and reality.
- **Kahneman's Basic Algorithm** — Kahneman observed that simple, transparent algorithms — often just a handful of weighted factors — frequently outperform expert intuition and even complex models. The classic example: a crude predictor like relationship longevity ≈ (frequency of sex – frequency of fights) outperforms professional counselors. — Humans overfit: we overweight anecdotes, moods, and noise. Complex models overfit too. Simple algorithms generalize better: a few robust inputs capture most of the predictive signal. It reframes prediction as: better to be roughly right with a rule than precisely wrong with an expert.
- **Uncertainty vs. Risk Analysis** — Risk is when you can attach probabilities to outcomes. Uncertainty is when you can't. Taleb emphasizes that most of life — especially fat-tailed events — lies in uncertainty, not risk. — Risk = roulette wheel: known odds, bounded downside. Uncertainty = real life: odds unknown, payoffs extreme. It forces humility: don't over-model the unmodelable. Risk = variance inside a model; uncertainty = model error itself (wrong distribution, fat tails); uncertainty dominates when small probabilities × huge consequences = ruin.
- **What You Need to Believe** — 'What You Need to Believe' (WYNTB) is a strategic and investment framework that asks: what specific assumptions must hold true for this business, trade, or thesis to succeed? Instead of getting lost in details, you identify the critical drivers that make or break the outcome. — Clarifies hidden assumptions; distills complex forecasts into a few 'keystone' variables. It focuses debate and diligence on the few numbers that matter most. Outcome = f(x1, x2, ..., xn); WYNTB asks which variables dominate (e.g., doubling LTV or halving CAC).
- **Signpost <> Bayes Factor** — A signpost is an event or piece of information that lets you update your model or gain/lose confidence in a prediction. The Bayes Factor (BF = P(Data|H1)/P(Data|H0)) measures how much evidence new data provides for one hypothesis versus another. BF>1 favors your thesis, BF<1 favors the alternative; log2(BF) gives the bits of evidence added, tying into edge and Kelly sizing. — Markets are hypothesis contests: consensus sets prices while you hold a contrarian thesis. Every earnings report, macro print, or channel check is a test of whether reality looks more like your thesis or consensus. The Bayes Factor is the disciplined way to update conviction instead of relying on vague feelings.
- **Expected Value** — Expected Value (EV) is the weighted average of possible outcomes, adjusted for probabilities. EV = Σ (Probability × Payoff). Investment cases often modeled as a "fan of outcomes." — Focuses decisions on process, not outcome. A single bad draw doesn't invalidate a positive-EV decision.
- **Brier Score** — The Brier Score is a measure of the accuracy of probabilistic forecasts. It rewards being confidently right and punishes being confidently wrong, using squared error. — Good for calibration: "Do my 70% predictions come true ~70% of the time?" It creates a single metric across many forecasts. Limitation: it rewards being close to consensus, even if consensus is mispriced — making it a poor tool for investors.
