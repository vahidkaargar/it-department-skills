# Bias Catalogue — 95 cognitive biases and tendencies (from the Behavioral Economics lattice)

Grouped by family. Each entry: **Bias** — definition (why it fires). Use this to match biases to a live decision. Do not apply all 95 — select the few that genuinely bite.

A note on scope: this is a practitioner's lattice, not a textbook taxonomy. Some entries are strictly biases, others are tendencies, emotions, or mechanisms (Nudging, Envy, Hedonic Treadmill) that distort decisions the same way. A few (Priming, Willpower Constraint) rest on contested replication evidence — treat them as weaker priors, useful as prompts rather than proof.

## Numerical Blindspots (15)
- **Anchoring Bias** — Initial numbers (anchors) heavily influence later judgments, even if irrelevant. (The mind latches onto the first reference point and adjusts insufficiently. It shows how **context shapes judgment**, not just facts.)
- **Anecdotal Evidence Bias** — Giving excessive weight to single vivid examples instead of broad data. (Humans are wired to remember stories, not statistics. It explains why “my uncle smoked and lived to 90” outweighs CDC data in persuasion.)
- **Base Rate Neglect** — Base rate neglect happens when people ignore general statistical information (“the base rate”) in favor of vivid, specific, or anecdotal evidence. (Humans are story-driven. We overweight the immediate or the particular and underweight the statistical background. It explains why smart people misjudge risk, overestimate rare events, and chase flashy opportunities.)
- **Biased Sample Neglect** — People assume their sample is unbiased when in fact it systematically excludes key groups. (Sampling bias masquerades as truth. It reminds us to ask: *who’s missing from the data?*)
- **Black Swan Neglect** — People dismiss or underweight rare, extreme-impact events. (The brain focuses on the “typical,” not the fat-tail events that define history. It highlights **impact ≠ frequency**.)
- **Causation versus Correlation** — Mistaking correlation between two variables as a causal link. (The brain craves explanation and invents causality. It shows how our need for narrative distorts inference.)
- **Compounding Neglect** — People underestimate how growth (or decay) compounds over time, assuming linear accumulation instead. (The human mind thinks additively, but reality multiplies. This neglect leads to under-preparation for exponential dynamics. It helps explain why people miss turning points until too late.)
- **Constant Correlation Fallacy** — Assuming correlations are stable and permanent when they may shift under new conditions. (We reify historical relationships as laws, forgetting that correlation is context-dependent. It emphasizes the fragility of statistical relationships.)
- **Data Mining Fallacy** — Finding "significant" results in large data by sheer chance. (With enough variables, you'll find spurious patterns. It highlights how modern data abundance breeds illusions.)
- **Decision Tree Alternate Path Neglect** — People fail to consider alternate branches in a decision tree, focusing on only one imagined path. (The mind linearizes uncertainty, imagining a single outcome stream. Strategic thinking requires holding multiple possibilities at once.)
- **Hidden Variable Bias** — Overlooking third factors that explain an observed relationship. (We leap to direct causality, ignoring lurking confounders. It guards against oversimplified reasoning.)
- **Law of Small Numbers** — Over-interpreting small samples as representative. (Humans expect randomness to "look" balanced. It explains why we see patterns in small streaks.)
- **Linearity Imposition Bias** — People assume relationships are linear when they may be curved, exponential, or S-shaped. (Humans simplify messy dynamics by forcing them into straight lines. It explains persistent forecasting errors when systems are nonlinear.)
- **Small Sample Neglect** — People assume small samples are representative of the whole population, ignoring variance. (We confuse noise for signal. It explains why early data, anecdotes, or first impressions carry outsized influence.)
- **Unknown Unknown Neglect** — People fail to consider possibilities outside their modeled set — 'we don't know what we don't know.' (The brain narrows the universe to only foreseeable risks. It forces humility in planning.)

## Certainty Bias (10)
- **Deterministic Thinking** — We see outcomes as inevitable and linear, ignoring chance and complexity. (Illusion of inevitability crowds out randomness. It explains why hindsight feels fated and forecasts feel overconfident.)
- **Doubt Avoidance** — Uncertainty creates discomfort, so we rush to eliminate it — often by prematurely deciding. (We prefer a bad answer to no answer. It explains impulsive closure in ambiguous environments.)
- **False Patterns** — We see patterns in randomness, mistaking noise for signal. (Humans are pattern-seeking machines; we impose structure where none exists. It explains superstition, overfitting, and conspiracy theories.)
- **False Precision Bias** — We trust exact numbers even when the underlying data or model is highly uncertain. (Specificity feels authoritative — "3.14159" seems more true than "about 3." It creates misplaced confidence in models, forecasts, and metrics.)
- **Identity Jenga** — When new information threatens core parts of our identity, we avoid updating beliefs — like a Jenga tower, pulling the wrong block risks collapse. (The fear of unraveling identity is stronger than the pull of truth. It explains resistance to evidence not by logic, but by self-preservation.)
- **Jumping to Conclusions** — We form judgments too quickly, before gathering enough evidence. (Speed over accuracy. It explains snap judgments and misreads that shape decisions disproportionately.)
- **Ludic Fallacy** — We mistake well-structured games with clear probabilities for messy real-world uncertainty. (The world is not a casino. It highlights how simplified models mislead when applied outside controlled domains.)
- **Outcome Bias** — A concept popularized by poker player Annie Duke: people judge decisions by their outcomes instead of the quality of the decision-making process. (Good decisions can lead to bad outcomes (because of chance), and bad decisions can lead to good outcomes (because of luck). Resulting is dangerous because it confuses skill with outcome variance.)
- **Positive Test Strategy** — We test hypotheses by seeking confirming evidence instead of trying to disprove them. (We look for validation, not falsification. It explains why bad ideas persist: they're only tested on their strengths.)
- **Value Seeking Behavior** — Value-seeking behavior is the tendency of value investors to chase cheapness rather than expected value. It's the behavioral mirror of risk-seeking — instead of reaching for volatility, value-seekers reach for 'optical value.' They're willing to lose money (or miss opportunity) to feel right about owning something that looks cheap. (What begins as discipline ('buy $1 for $0.70') becomes ideology ('buy low P/E, low P/B'). The investor optimizes for valuation comfort instead of return on capital. Cheapness often signals business decay, not mispricing. Expected Return = (Probability of Re-Rating × Upside) – (Probability of Decline × Downside); value-seekers focus only on price while ignoring quality, durability, growth.)

## Consistency Bias (8)
- **Bias Towards Orderly Solution** — We prefer simple, neat, and structured explanations — even when reality is messy. (Order feels like truth. It explains why humans oversimplify complexity.)
- **Broken Window** — Visible disorder signals permissibility of further disorder. (Chaos compounds. It shows how environment cues amplify behavior.)
- **Cognitive Dissonance** — Discomfort from holding contradictory beliefs or actions pushes us to rationalize. (We'd rather distort reality than live with contradiction. It explains motivated reasoning at a deep level.)
- **Consistency** — Humans feel an overwhelming need to appear consistent with their past words, beliefs, and actions — even when circumstances change. Once we take a public stance, our brains work hard to justify it, often distorting reality to stay consistent. (Consistency acts like mental gravity: it anchors behavior and beliefs. This can be good (reliability, trust) but dangerous when it locks us into bad decisions. It explains why individuals and organizations double down instead of pivoting.)
- **Effort Justification Bias** — The more effort we put into something, the more we value it — regardless of intrinsic worth. (Pain becomes proof of value. It explains inflated attachment to costly efforts.)
- **First Conclusion Tendency** — We seize on the first plausible explanation and resist revisiting it. (Speed masquerades as certainty. It explains why premature closure distorts reasoning.)
- **Model Blindness** — We stop questioning the limits of a model once it explains enough, and begin mistaking the map for the territory. (A model's usefulness becomes its blindfold. It warns that success with one framework makes us ignore alternative explanations.)
- **Sunk Cost Fallacy** — We continue investing in failing endeavors because of resources already spent. (Past costs bias future choices, even though they're unrecoverable. It explains wasteful persistence.)

## Mimetic Tendency (7)
- **Adaptation Principle** — We quickly adjust to new circumstances, good or bad. (Human satisfaction returns to baseline after shocks. It predicts the limited long-term effect of external changes.)
- **Dominance Hierarchy** — Humans structure themselves into implicit hierarchies of rank and power. (The brain tracks relative position continuously. It shapes decisions in ways unrelated to objective outcomes.)
- **Envy** — Negative emotion triggered by others' success or advantage. (Envy acts as a motivational force but can also be destructive. It explains sabotage, resentment, and positional arms races.)
- **Hedonic Treadmill** — Pleasure gains wear off, leading to endless pursuit of more. (Relative comparisons and adaptation drive constant striving. It explains why rising wealth doesn’t guarantee rising happiness.)
- **Imitation** — We learn behaviors by copying others — especially peers and high-status individuals. (Imitation is the baseline human learning algorithm. It explains rapid diffusion of behaviors without conscious reasoning.)
- **Jealousy** — Fear of losing valued relationships to rivals. (Jealousy is an evolved guard against mate-poaching and betrayal. It explains protective and sometimes destructive behavior.)
- **Mimetic Desire** — We desire things because others desire them. (Value is not intrinsic but relational. It explains rivalry, envy, and bubbles.)

## Association (5)
- **Associative Shotgun** — Brain triggers multiple loosely related associations at once, leading to noise in judgment. (Our networked minds prefer connections over precision. It shows how irrelevant associations distort clarity.)
- **Nudging** — Structuring choices to steer decisions without removing options. (Humans are predictably irrational, so choice architecture matters. It formalizes small interventions as powerful levers.)
- **Pavlovian Association** — Automatic linking of stimuli and responses through repeated pairing. (We are conditioned animals. It explains gut-level reactions outside rational thought.)
- **Priming** — Subtle exposure to stimuli influences later behavior or decisions. (Unconscious nudges shape conscious thought. It shows how small cues create large downstream effects.)
- **Secondary Reinforcers** — In psychology, a secondary reinforcer is a stimulus that gains motivational value by being repeatedly associated with a primary reinforcer (something that directly stimulates the brain's pleasure/reward system). Rewards are stronger when the stimulus-to-pleasure link is immediate, powerful, and tightly linked in context; over time the brain associates the surrounding context (brand, packaging, ritual) with pleasure, leading to habit, brand loyalty, and pricing power. (Stronger and more immediate reinforcement produces more habitual, sticky consumer behavior and higher loyalty; delayed reinforcement produces weaker association and lower loyalty. This explains why some consumer products carry far higher margins than others despite similar cost structures.)

## Flashlight (5)
- **Framing** — Decisions change based on how information is presented, even if content is identical. (People don't respond to objective states, but to perceived reference frames. It exposes context as a lever.)
- **What is Focal is Causal** — We assume the visible actor caused the outcome. (Attention drives attribution. It explains misassignment of causality in complex systems.)
- **What is Focal is Important** — People equate prominence with importance. (The spotlight effect distorts judgment. It shows how salience ≠ significance.)
- **What is Measured Matters** — Focus skews toward metrics being tracked, regardless of relevance. (Metrics create incentives and blind spots. It explains Goodhart's Law: 'When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.')
- **What You See is All There Is** — We judge based only on available evidence, ignoring missing data (WYSIATI). (The mind fills gaps with overconfidence. It highlights how blind spots create illusions of completeness.)

## Availability (4)
- **Availability Bias** — We judge frequency or probability by how easily examples come to mind. (Ease of recall ≠ actual likelihood. It shapes intuitive probability judgments.)
- **Recency Bias** — Recency Bias is the tendency to overweight recent events when judging probabilities, risks, or performance. The near past feels more relevant than the distant past, even when the long-term average tells a different story. (The brain is wired for availability: what's fresh feels more likely. It explains why people extrapolate from the latest trend and ignore mean reversion or historical cycles.)
- **Skeuomorphism** — Skeuomorphism means carrying over old forms into new domains even when they are no longer necessary (the trash-can icon, faux-leather textures). As an investor or strategist, it is treating a new technology, market, or business model as if it must follow the rules and appearance of the old one. (We cling to the old frame because it feels familiar, not because it fits. Skeuomorphism is a transition bias - new systems often masquerade as the old before developing their native form, and investors who confuse the imitation phase for the final form miss transformative value or misprice risk.)
- **Vivid Evidence** — We overweight memorable, emotionally charged examples over statistical base rates. (The mind prefers stories to statistics. It distorts risk perception. Decision Weight ≈ P(Event) + γ × Vividness with γ > 0 amplifying emotional salience.)

## Lies (4)
- **Denial** — Rejecting or minimizing reality that conflicts with emotional comfort or identity. (Defense mechanism against psychological pain. It explains persistence of false beliefs even in face of overwhelming evidence.)
- **Repetition** — Repeated exposure increases familiarity and acceptance. (The "mere exposure effect" turns the familiar into the credible. It explains preference formation without rational basis (returns diminish but bias persists).)
- **Self Lies** — We deceive ourselves to maintain coherence, pride, or motivation. (Self-deception smooths over contradictions. It preserves short-term functioning at the cost of long-term clarity (Belief = Truth x (1-lambda) + Comfort x lambda).)
- **Willful Blindness** — Choosing not to see or investigate inconvenient truths. (Ignorance is a strategy: by 'not knowing,' people avoid responsibility or cognitive dissonance. It explains systemic oversight failures. Perceived Cost = Cost(Truth) − Cost(Ignorance); if Cost(Truth) > Cost(Ignorance), blindness is incentivized.)

## Deprival (4)
- **Deprival Superresponse** — Losses loom larger than gains; we overreact when something is taken away. (The fear of losing what's already ours drives disproportionate behaviors. It explains escalation beyond rational value.)
- **Loss Aversion** — Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good. (The asymmetry in emotional weight makes people risk-averse in gains and risk-seeking in losses. It predicts consistent deviations from rational utility theory.)
- **Scarcity** — Limited availability increases perceived value (perceived value is roughly inversely proportional to supply). (The brain equates scarcity with importance and opportunity cost. It explains demand spikes independent of utility.)
- **Territorial Superresponse** — Humans (and animals) defend territory with intensity far beyond its objective value (Utility(Territory) = Value + k x Defense Premium, where k is much greater than 1 once a boundary is threatened). (Loss of territory threatens identity, autonomy, and future options. It explains disproportionate conflicts over 'space,' whether physical or symbolic.)

## Affect Bias (4)
- **Disliking** — The inverse of liking: we undervalue or dismiss information from people we dislike. (Negative affect contaminates judgment. It explains both irrational rejection of truth and entrenched polarization.)
- **Fundamental Attribution Error** — We over-attribute others' actions to character while underweighting situational factors. Think in terms of conditional probability: P(Behavior) = P(Trait) + P(Situation). We overweight P(Trait) and underweight P(Situation), leading to miscalibrated inferences. (The bias arises because personal traits are salient while context is invisible. It explains systematic misjudgments of responsibility and competence.)
- **Halo Effect** — A single positive trait (attractiveness, intelligence, success) colors overall judgment. (We extrapolate from the visible to the invisible. It creates systematic overestimation of global competence from local attributes.)
- **Liking** — We judge ideas, products, or people more favorably when we like the person or source. (Positive affect bleeds into evaluation. It shows how unrelated emotions bias rational judgment.)

## Self-Regard (4)
- **Endowment Effect** — We value things we already own more highly than equivalent things we don't. (Ownership changes perception: we assign intrinsic value to what's mine. It explains reluctance to trade even when rational, creating sticky assets and inefficiencies.)
- **False Consensus** — We assume others share our beliefs, values, or preferences more than they actually do. (Humans project their worldview outward. It explains miscommunication, tribal misunderstandings, and why groups are often shocked by "unexpected" election results.)
- **Illusion of Control** — We believe we can influence outcomes that are largely determined by chance. (The brain over-ascribes causality to our actions. It explains gambling behavior, superstition, and overconfident management.)
- **Overoptimism** — We systematically overestimate the likelihood of good outcomes and underestimate risks. (Optimism is adaptive (it fuels persistence), but it blinds us to base rates and downsides. It explains why most people think they'll do better than average, and why projects constantly go over budget and schedule.)

## Confirmation (3)
- **Believing Machine** — Humans are wired to believe first, doubt later (if at all). (Acceptance is cognitively cheaper than skepticism. It explains susceptibility to misinformation.)
- **Confirmation Bias** — We search for, interpret, and recall information that confirms preexisting beliefs. (Preserves cognitive consistency at the cost of accuracy. It skews learning loops.)
- **Elephant's Lawyer** — Reasoning often acts as a lawyer defending subconscious intuitions (the elephant), not as a neutral judge. (We rationalize what we already feel. It explains motivated reasoning and confirmation-seeking.)

## Glucose (3)
- **Cognitive Fatigue** — Prolonged mental effort reduces performance, attention, and accuracy. (Brains tire like bodies. It explains why good reasoning declines even with expertise.)
- **Cognitive Load** — Our brains have a limited working memory capacity, and overload degrades reasoning. (Too much input clogs cognition. It reveals why multitasking collapses clarity.)
- **Willpower Constraint** — Self-control is a finite resource that depletes with use. (Willpower is like a muscle — strong but exhaustible. It explains why discipline wanes over the day.)

## Story (3)
- **Coherent Narrative Confidence** — Once a story feels coherent, people gain unwarranted confidence in its truth. (Coherence is not accuracy. Our minds reward smooth, consistent explanations with certainty, even when the explanation is false. It explains why false but tidy theories outperform messy truths.)
- **Narrative Instinct** — Humans naturally organize information into stories with beginnings, middles, and ends. (Stories are powerful because they compress complexity into causal arcs. It explains both why stories transmit culture and why they distort reality (forcing order onto randomness).)
- **Primitive Moral Reasoning** — We default to moral categories like good/evil, us/them, fair/unfair when processing events. (Moral shortcuts simplify complex tradeoffs into emotional binaries. It explains both social cohesion and polarization.)

## Tribal (3)
- **Social Proof** — We look to others' behavior to decide what is correct. (Uncertainty drives imitation of the majority. It explains herding and conformity (the probability of adopting grows with the visible crowd).)
- **Tribal Identity Bias** — We favor our in-group and distrust out-groups. (Identity overrides evidence. It explains polarized reasoning. Belief Update = Evidence × (1 if from In-group, <1 if from Out-group).)
- **Tribal Incentive Bias** — When group incentives (status, rewards, cohesion) dominate, people distort truth or suppress dissent to stay aligned. (It's not just identity, but material and reputational payoffs tied to group alignment. It explains why even intelligent individuals reinforce faulty group narratives. Expected Utility = Personal Payoff + Group Payoff × Weight; often Group Payoff ≫ Truth Payoff → distortion becomes rational.)

## Comparison (2)
- **Boiling Frog** — Failure to perceive slow, incremental changes until it’s too late. (Humans react to sudden shocks, not gradual drifts. It explains why creeping risks go unnoticed.)
- **Contrast-Misreaction Tendency** — Humans evaluate things relatively, not absolutely. We react to differences, not levels. Our judgment is distorted by what something is compared to, not what it is in isolation. (Our brains anchor on contrast — light vs. dark, cheap vs. expensive, safe vs. dangerous. A mediocre option looks great next to a terrible one. A high price seems fair after seeing a higher reference price. It explains why people, markets, and organizations often chase illusions of value.)

## Substitution Bias (2)
- **Easy Question** — When faced with a hard question, we unconsciously answer an easier one instead. (The mind substitutes without noticing. It shows a hidden mechanism behind bad reasoning.)
- **Proxy Metric** — Using an imperfect measure as a stand-in for the real target. (We collapse complexity into easier metrics. It shows how proxies mislead if mistaken for the thing itself.)

## Reciprocity (2)
- **Fairness** — We prefer outcomes perceived as fair, even at cost to ourselves. Utility = Own Payoff − λ·(Perceived Inequity). (Fairness instinct stabilizes cooperation. It explains costly punishment and altruism.)
- **Reciprocity** — We return favors - both positive and negative. (Reciprocity enforces social contracts without central authority. It predicts cycles of cooperation or retaliation.)

## Incentive (2)
- **Incentive Bias** — We overweight evidence and reasoning that aligns with our incentives. (“Incentives are superpowers” (Munger). It explains systemic distortions whenever rewards are misaligned with truth.)
- **Maslow's Hammer** — When you only have one tool, every problem looks like a nail. (Cognitive laziness prefers overusing familiar solutions. It shows how expertise can blind as much as it enlightens.)

## Authority (2)
- **Obedience** — Humans have a strong tendency to obey authority figures, even when instructions conflict with personal conscience. (Obedience is not just respect for rules but a deep, automatic deference to authority. The Milgram experiments showed ordinary people administered what they thought were lethal shocks when directed by an experimenter in a lab coat. It reveals how authority bypasses moral checks.)
- **Psychological Reactance** — When freedom feels threatened, we push back - even irrationally. (Attempts to restrict choice often produce opposite behavior. It explains resistance to persuasion.)

## Groupthink (1)
- **Groupthink** — Groupthink happens when a group prioritizes harmony and consensus over critical evaluation. Dissenting voices are suppressed, and the group convinces itself it is making rational decisions when in fact it's avoiding conflict. Group Decision Quality = Individual Signal Strength - Conformity Pressure; as conformity pressure rises, signal collapses into an echo chamber. (Humans are tribal. In groups, the desire to belong and avoid friction can override the desire for truth. It explains catastrophic failures where no one wanted to be the squeaky wheel.)

## Hindsight Bias (1)
- **Hindsight Bias** — Hindsight Bias is the tendency to see events as having been predictable after they happen. Once outcomes are revealed, we reconstruct our memories and judgments to make the past seem more obvious than it really was. (The mind edits history: “I knew it all along.” This makes us overconfident in our ability to predict the future, underestimates uncertainty, and distorts lessons from experience. It’s it explains why people consistently mislearn from both wins and losses.)

## Reason-Respecting Tendency (1)
- **Reason-Respecting Tendency** — People comply more when given a reason, even if the reason is weak. (Our brain is tuned to process explanations as signals of legitimacy. It predicts persuasion power beyond actual logic.)
